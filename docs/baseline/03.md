# Day 1 — B0 Congestion and Shared-Relay Baseline

## Objective

The goal of today was to extend the B0 packet-level simulator so that it could model actual queue buildup and congestion under controlled traffic.

Previously, packets were processed mostly one at a time. Because of this, the queue implementation existed, but packets could not realistically accumulate while another packet was still being processed.

Today introduced simulated time, traffic generation, node service time, queue waiting-time measurements, and several controlled B0 congestion experiments.

B0 remains unchanged as the hop-count Dijkstra baseline.

---

# 1. Simulated Time

A discrete-event simulation engine was introduced.

Instead of using real waiting or `Thread.sleep()`, events are scheduled at simulated times.

Example:

```text
t = 0 → packet generated
t = 1 → another packet generated
t = 5 → relay finishes processing a packet
```

The simulation jumps directly between event times.

This allows long and heavy traffic experiments to run quickly and reproducibly.

The simulation engine also preserves deterministic ordering when multiple events occur at the same simulated time.

---

# 2. Traffic Generation

A traffic generator was introduced to automatically create packets at controlled intervals.

For example:

```text
count = 4
startTime = 0
interval = 2
```

produces:

```text
MSG-0 at t=0
MSG-1 at t=2
MSG-2 at t=4
MSG-3 at t=6
```

This makes packet arrival rate an experimental parameter.

---

# 3. Relay Service Time

A simulated service node was introduced.

A relay can now require a fixed amount of simulated time to process each packet.

Example:

```text
serviceTime = 3
```

with packets arriving at:

```text
t=0
t=1
t=2
```

produced:

```text
MSG-0 completed at t=3
MSG-1 completed at t=6
MSG-2 completed at t=9
```

Observed result:

```text
===== SERVICE NODE TEST =====
MSG-0 completed at t=3
MSG-1 completed at t=6
MSG-2 completed at t=9
Processed packets: 3
Dropped packets: 0
=============================
```

This confirmed that the node could process packets more slowly than packets arrive.

---

# 4. Queue Overflow Test

A controlled queue-overflow scenario was then tested.

Configuration:

```text
Queue capacity = 2
Service time = 5
Packets generated = 5
Arrival interval = 1
```

Observed result:

```text
===== QUEUE OVERFLOW TEST =====
MSG-0 completed at t=5
MSG-1 completed at t=10
MSG-2 completed at t=15
Processed packets: 3
Dropped packets: 2
Remaining queued packets: 0
===============================
```

This demonstrated a new failure type:

```text
Earlier:
packet drop because wireless retries failed

Today:
packet drop because relay queue was full
```

Therefore, the simulator can now distinguish link-related failure from congestion-related failure.

---

# 5. Congestion Measurements

The service-node telemetry was extended to measure:

```text
maximum queue occupancy
average queue waiting time
maximum queue waiting time
processed packets
dropped packets
```

Queue waiting time means the amount of simulated time between packet arrival and the moment the relay begins processing that packet.

For example:

```text
packet arrives at t=4
processing begins at t=18

queue waiting time = 14
```

Average queue waiting time represents the mean waiting time across processed packets.

Maximum queue waiting time represents the worst waiting time observed for a processed packet.

---

# 6. B0 Congestion Experiment

A topology containing a short B0 path and a longer alternative was used.

```text
A → B → D
```

Cost:

```text
2 hops
```

Alternative:

```text
A → C → E → D
```

Cost:

```text
3 hops
```

Because B0 uses hop count only, it selected:

```text
A → B → D
```

Relay B was configured with:

```text
Queue capacity = 3
Service time = 5
```

Ten packets were generated at an interval of one simulated time unit.

Observed result:

```text
===== B0 CONGESTION EXPERIMENT =====
Selected B0 route: [A, B, D]
Generated packets: 10
Processed at relay B: 5
Dropped at relay B: 5
Relay acceptance ratio: 50.0%
Processed packet IDs: [MSG-0, MSG-1, MSG-2, MSG-3, MSG-6]
Final B queue size: 0
Max queue size: 3
Average queue wait: 7.6
Max queue wait: 14
Route requests: 1
Cache hits: 0
Cache misses: 1
Dijkstra calculations: 1
====================================
```

Under this controlled configuration, B processed only half of the generated packets.

The queue reached its full capacity of three.

Processed packets waited an average of:

```text
7.6 simulated time units
```

and the worst processed packet waited:

```text
14 simulated time units
```

before processing began.

This result shows that congestion affected both reliability and delay.

# 7. Traffic Load Sweep

The next experiment varied packet arrival rate while keeping relay B unchanged.

Fixed relay parameters:

```text
Service time = 5
Queue capacity = 3
Packets = 10
```

Only the arrival interval changed.

Observed result:

```text
===== B0 CONGESTION LOAD SWEEP =====
Interval | Generated | Processed | Dropped | MaxQueue | AvgWait | MaxWait
6 | 10 | 10 | 0 | 1 | 0.0 | 0
5 | 10 | 10 | 0 | 1 | 0.0 | 0
3 | 10 | 9 | 1 | 3 | 7.666666666666667 | 14
1 | 10 | 5 | 5 | 3 | 7.6 | 14
====================================
```

## Interpretation

When:

```text
arrival interval = 6
service time = 5
```

the relay processed packets faster than new packets arrived.

No queue buildup or packet loss occurred.

At:

```text
arrival interval = 5
```

the arrival rate approximately matched the relay service rate.

No packet loss occurred.

At:

```text
arrival interval = 3
```

packets arrived faster than the relay could process them.

The queue reached full capacity and one packet was dropped.

At:

```text
arrival interval = 1
```

the relay was heavily overloaded.

Five of ten packets were dropped.

This experiment shows a transition from stable operation to queue saturation as offered traffic increases.

# 8. Shared-Relay Multi-Flow Experiment

The next experiment tested several simultaneous B0 flows sharing one relay.

Topology:

```text
A ─┐
C ─┼── B ── D
E ─┤
F ─┘
```

Each source had a shortest path to D through B.

Each source generated:

```text
5 packets
```

at:

```text
1 packet every 6 simulated time units
```

Relay B had:

```text
Service time = 5
Queue capacity = 5
```

One source alone could therefore generate traffic slowly enough for B to process it.

However, four simultaneous sources created a much larger combined arrival rate.

Observed result:

```text
===== B0 MULTI-FLOW CONGESTION =====
Sources: [A, C, E, F]
Packets per source: 5
Generated packets: 20
Processed at relay B: 11
Dropped at relay B: 9
Drop rate: 45.0%
Max queue size: 5
Average queue wait: 7.2727272727272725
Max queue wait: 24
Route requests: 4
Cache hits: 0
Cache misses: 4
Dijkstra calculations: 4
====================================
```

The relay processed:

```text
11 / 20 packets
```

and dropped:

```text
9 / 20 packets
```

giving:

```text
45% queue-related packet loss
```

The maximum queue occupancy reached its configured capacity of five.

The worst accepted packet waited:

```text
24 simulated time units
```

before processing.

The routing cache showed four misses because four different source-destination route keys were requested:

```text
A → D
C → D
E → D
F → D
```

Therefore, the four Dijkstra calculations are expected in this experiment and do not indicate a cache problem.

---

# 9. Source-Count Sweep

The final experiment varied only the number of simultaneous sources.

The following parameters remained fixed:

```text
5 packets per source
arrival interval = 6
relay service time = 5
relay queue capacity = 5
```

The number of sources increased from one to four.

Observed result:

```text
===== B0 MULTI-FLOW SOURCE SWEEP =====
Sources | Generated | Processed | Dropped | DropRate | MaxQueue | AvgWait | MaxWait
1 | 5 | 5 | 0 | 0.0% | 1 | 0.0 | 0
2 | 10 | 10 | 0 | 0.0% | 5 | 4.0 | 15
3 | 15 | 11 | 4 | 26.666666666666668% | 5 | 6.181818181818182 | 22
4 | 20 | 11 | 9 | 45.0% | 5 | 7.2727272727272725 | 24
======================================
```

## Interpretation

### One source

With one source:

```text
Generated = 5
Processed = 5
Dropped = 0
Max queue = 1
Average wait = 0
```

The relay handled the traffic comfortably.

### Two sources

With two simultaneous sources:

```text
Generated = 10
Processed = 10
Dropped = 0
Max queue = 5
Average wait = 4.0
Max wait = 15
```

No packets were dropped, but the relay queue reached full capacity.

This is important because it shows that congestion pressure can appear before packet loss begins.

The network still delivered all relay-accepted traffic, but packets experienced increasing queue delay.

### Three sources

With three simultaneous sources:

```text
Generated = 15
Processed = 11
Dropped = 4
Drop rate ≈ 26.67%
Average wait ≈ 6.18
Max wait = 22
```

At this point the combined traffic exceeded the relay's ability to absorb the workload.

Queue saturation resulted in packet loss.

### Four sources

With four simultaneous sources:

```text
Generated = 20
Processed = 11
Dropped = 9
Drop rate = 45%
Average wait ≈ 7.27
Max wait = 24
```

The relay remained saturated and packet loss became severe.

---

# 10. Main Observation

The strongest observation from today's experiments is that individually reasonable traffic flows can collectively overload a shared shortest-path relay.

Under the fixed configuration:

```text
Per-source packet interval = 6
Relay service time = 5
Relay queue capacity = 5
```

one source generated no congestion.

Two sources generated queue pressure and delay but no packet loss.

Three sources caused the first substantial packet loss.

Four sources produced a 45% drop rate.

The observed transition was:

```text
1 source
→ healthy

2 sources
→ queue saturation pressure, but no drops

3 sources
→ packet loss begins

4 sources
→ severe congestion and increased delay
```

This is a useful B0 baseline result because hop-count routing considers route length but does not currently include relay queue load in its path-selection metric.

---

# 11. Important Interpretation

The result should not be stated as:

```text
"B0 always causes 45% packet loss."
```

That would be incorrect.

The correct interpretation is:

> Under the specific controlled topology, traffic rate, service time, and queue capacity used in this experiment, increasing the number of B0 flows sharing one relay caused queue saturation, increased waiting time, and eventually packet loss.

The exact threshold depends on the experiment configuration.

---

# 12. Congestion Before Packet Loss

One important finding appeared in the two-source case.

The result was:

```text
Dropped packets = 0
Max queue size = 5
Average waiting time = 4
Maximum waiting time = 15
```

This means packet loss was still zero even though the queue was already saturated.

Therefore, packet drops are not the first indication of congestion.

Queue occupancy and queue waiting time can provide earlier warning signals.

This observation may become relevant later when designing multi-metric routing because queue state can potentially be considered before a relay begins dropping packets.

This is only a motivation for later investigation and is not yet evidence that a queue-aware routing protocol will outperform B0.

# 15. Current Limitations

The current congestion experiment focuses mainly on the service behavior of the shared relay.

`Processed at relay B` means that B successfully accepted and processed the packet.

It does not yet represent complete end-to-end delivery through every simulated hop to destination D.

Future experiments should extend the event-based simulator so that packet processing at B is followed by actual forwarding to the next hop and final destination.

The simulator also currently uses controlled deterministic traffic and service-time parameters.

These are useful for reproducibility and correctness but are not direct measurements of real Android Bluetooth timing.

Real-device behavior must be measured separately during the final validation phase.

---

# 16. Next Steps

The next stage should extend the event-based simulation toward complete end-to-end network measurements.

The next important additions are:

```text
end-to-end packet delivery
delivery timestamp
end-to-end latency
queue-delay contribution
throughput
destination-level PDR
```

After that, the next B0 network conditions should include dynamic link failure, topology change, mobility, and partition/reconnection.

Once the B0 behavior is sufficiently characterized, the next protocol phase can introduce dynamic multi-metric routing.

That phase can investigate whether metrics such as:

```text
link reliability
expected retransmission cost
queue load
queue delay
link freshness
link stability
```

can improve routing decisions relative to hop-count-only B0 under the same controlled scenarios.

# Day Summary

Today's goal was to make the queue model experimentally meaningful.

That goal was achieved.

The simulator moved from:

```text
packet 1 finishes
then packet 2
then packet 3
```

to:

```text
packets arrive over simulated time
        ↓
relay processes at a limited rate
        ↓
packets accumulate
        ↓
queue delay increases
        ↓
queue reaches capacity
        ↓
packets are dropped
```

The most important controlled result was the source-count sweep:

```text
Sources | Drop Rate | Avg Wait | Max Wait
1       | 0%        | 0.0      | 0
2       | 0%        | 4.0      | 15
3       | 26.67%    | 6.18     | 22
4       | 45%       | 7.27     | 24
```

This establishes a reproducible B0 shared-relay congestion baseline for the later protocol-comparison phase.
