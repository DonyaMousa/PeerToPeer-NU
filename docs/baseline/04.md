# Day 3 — End-to-End Delivery, Latency, Throughput, and Shared-Relay Congestion

## Objective

Extend the timed congestion simulator from relay-level processing to full destination-level delivery.

Before today, a packet being successfully processed at relay B was counted only as relay success.

Today the simulator was extended so that packets continue from the relay to destination D, allowing direct measurement of:

- destination-level packet delivery ratio (PDR)
- end-to-end latency
- average latency
- median latency
- maximum latency
- throughput
- queue waiting time
- queue occupancy
- congestion-related packet drops

The B0 routing baseline itself was not changed.

B0 still represents hop-count Dijkstra routing with no awareness of queue load, link reliability, or congestion.

---

# 1. Timed Delivery Result

A new timed delivery result model was introduced.

Each packet can now record:

```text
messageId
createdAt
deliveredAt
delivered
dropped
```

For delivered packets:

```text
end-to-end latency
=
deliveredAt - createdAt
```

Example:

```text
createdAt = 2
deliveredAt = 15

latency = 13 simulated time units
```

Dropped packets do not have a delivery timestamp or delivery latency.

---

# 2. End-to-End Timed Forwarding

The timed forwarding simulation was extended from:

```text
A → B
```

to:

```text
A
↓
B receives packet
↓
packet may wait in B queue
↓
B processes packet
↓
B forwards toward D
↓
D receives packet
↓
delivery timestamp recorded
```

The current controlled forwarding model uses:

```text
relay service time = configurable
relay-to-destination delay = configurable
```

For the basic correctness test:

```text
relay service time = 5
B → D delay = 2
```

A packet created at `t = 0` was delivered at `t = 7`, giving end-to-end latency of 7 simulated time units.

---

# 3. Queue Waiting and End-to-End Latency

The next test sent multiple packets through the same relay.

Packets arrived at:

```text
MSG-0 → t=0
MSG-1 → t=1
MSG-2 → t=2
```

Relay B required 5 simulated time units to process each packet.

Expected relay completion times:

```text
MSG-0 → t=5
MSG-1 → t=10
MSG-2 → t=15
```

With an additional relay-to-destination delay of 2 simulated time units, the packets reached D at:

```text
MSG-0 → t=7
MSG-1 → t=12
MSG-2 → t=17
```

Their end-to-end latencies were:

```text
MSG-0 → 7
MSG-1 → 11
MSG-2 → 15
```

This demonstrated:

```text
more queue waiting
→ higher end-to-end latency
```

---

# 4. Timed Network Telemetry

A destination-level telemetry component was introduced.

It aggregates timed delivery results and calculates:

```text
generated packets
delivered packets
dropped packets
packet delivery ratio
average latency
median latency
maximum latency
throughput
```

Packet delivery ratio is:

```text
PDR = delivered / generated
```

Throughput is currently represented as:

```text
delivered packets / simulated experiment duration
```

Therefore, throughput is measured in packets per simulated time unit. The simulated time unit is intentionally abstract at this stage and should not yet be interpreted as milliseconds or seconds.

# 5. B0 End-to-End Load Sweep

The main controlled experiment varied only the packet arrival interval.

Fixed relay configuration:

```text
serviceTime = 5
queueCapacity = 5
relay → destination delay = 2
packets = 10
```

Traffic intervals:

```text
Low traffic    → interval 6
Medium traffic → interval 3
Heavy traffic  → interval 1
```

Observed result:

```text
===== B0 END-TO-END LOAD SWEEP =====
Interval | Generated | Delivered | Dropped | PDR | AvgLatency | MedianLatency | MaxLatency | AvgQueueWait | MaxQueueWait
6 | 10 | 10 | 0 | 100.0% | 7.0 | 7.0 | 7 | 0.0 | 0
3 | 10 | 10 | 0 | 100.0% | 16.0 | 16.0 | 25 | 9.0 | 18
1 | 10 | 7 | 3 | 70.0% | 19.0 | 19.0 | 31 | 12.0 | 24
====================================
```

---

# 6. Interpretation of the Load Sweep

## Low traffic — interval 6

Observed:

```text
Generated = 10
Delivered = 10
Dropped = 0
PDR = 100%
Average latency = 7
Median latency = 7
Maximum latency = 7
Average queue wait = 0
Maximum queue wait = 0
```

This represents a healthy condition with no queue buildup.

## Medium traffic — interval 3

Observed:

```text
Generated = 10
Delivered = 10
Dropped = 0
PDR = 100%
Average latency = 16
Median latency = 16
Maximum latency = 25
Average queue wait = 9
Maximum queue wait = 18
```

PDR remains 100%, but latency and queue waiting rise substantially.

This is important because congestion is already present even though no packets are lost.

## Heavy traffic — interval 1

Observed:

```text
Generated = 10
Delivered = 7
Dropped = 3
PDR = 70%
Average latency = 19
Median latency = 19
Maximum latency = 31
Average queue wait = 12
Maximum queue wait = 24
```

At this point the relay is overloaded. Queue overflow produces packet loss and destination-level PDR falls.

The progression is:

```text
LOW LOAD
→ 100% PDR, no queueing, low latency

CONGESTED
→ 100% PDR, but high queueing and higher latency

OVERLOADED
→ queue overflow, packet loss, lower PDR
```

---

# 7. Main Finding from the Load Sweep

The most important observation is:

> Congestion becomes visible in queue waiting time and end-to-end latency before packet delivery ratio begins to fall.

At interval 3:

```text
PDR = 100%
Average queue wait = 9
Average latency = 16
Maximum latency = 25
```

Therefore, using only PDR as a network-health signal would miss an important congestion stage.

This does not yet prove that queue-aware routing will outperform B0. It establishes a baseline motivation for later testing.

# 8. End-to-End Shared-Relay Multi-Flow Experiment

The strongest experiment of Day 3 combined the shared-relay scenario with destination-level delivery measurements.

Topology:

```text
A ─┐
C ─┼── B ── D
E ─┤
F ─┘
```

All four sources send toward destination D through shared relay B.

Each source generated 5 packets, for a combined total of 20 packets.

Relay configuration:

```text
queue capacity = 5
service time = 5
relay → destination delay = 2
```

Packet IDs included source identity:

```text
MSG-A-0
MSG-C-0
MSG-E-0
MSG-F-0
...
```

This guarantees unique IDs across simultaneous flows and prevents cross-flow telemetry collisions.

Observed result:

```text
===== B0 END-TO-END MULTI-FLOW =====
Sources: [A, C, E, F]
Generated: 20
Delivered: 11
Dropped: 9
PDR: 55.00000000000001%
Average latency: 22.545454545454547
Median latency: 25.0
Max latency: 31
Average queue wait: 15.545454545454545
Max queue wait: 24
Max queue size: 5
====================================
```

For reporting:

```text
Generated = 20
Delivered = 11
Dropped = 9
PDR = 55%
Average latency ≈ 22.55
Median latency = 25
Maximum latency = 31
Average queue wait ≈ 15.55
Maximum queue wait = 24
Maximum queue size = 5
```

---

# 9. Interpretation of Shared-Relay Result

Four traffic flows converge on relay B:

```text
A ─┐
C ─┼→ B → D
E ─┤
F ─┘
```

The combined offered load exceeds B's service ability.

This causes:

```text
shared shortest-path relay
        ↓
queue growth
        ↓
queue reaches capacity
        ↓
packet waiting increases
        ↓
queue overflow occurs
        ↓
destination-level PDR falls
```

Only 11 of 20 packets reached destination D.

Therefore:

```text
PDR = 55%
Drop rate = 45%
```

Surviving packets also experienced substantial delay:

```text
Average end-to-end latency ≈ 22.55
Median latency = 25
Maximum latency = 31
```

---

# 10. Internal Consistency Check

The shared-relay experiment produced:

```text
Average queue wait ≈ 15.55
Average end-to-end latency ≈ 22.55
```

The difference is approximately 7.

The controlled forwarding model adds:

```text
relay service = 5
relay → destination delay = 2
```

Therefore:

```text
queue wait + controlled forwarding cost
≈ end-to-end latency
```

This confirms that the timing measurements are internally consistent with the simulation model.

# 13. Relationship to the B0 Baseline

B0 still selects routes using hop-count Dijkstra.

It does not currently use:

```text
queue occupancy
queue waiting time
relay load
expected retries
link quality
link stability
traffic concentration
```

Therefore, B0 can continue selecting the same shortest path even as the selected relay becomes congested.

The shared-relay result demonstrates this baseline limitation under a controlled scenario.

The result does not establish that DynaBLE-RX or any future adaptive routing mechanism is better. A fair comparison requires running the candidate protocol under the same topology, traffic, queue, and timing parameters.

---

# 14. Research Interpretation

A careful research statement from Day 3 is:

> Under the controlled B0 simulation, increasing offered traffic first increased queue waiting time and end-to-end latency while destination PDR remained at 100%. Under heavier load, queue overflow caused destination PDR to fall. In the four-source shared-relay scenario, 11 of 20 packets reached the destination, producing 55% PDR and approximately 22.55 simulated time units of average end-to-end latency.

A second useful observation is:

> Queue-related metrics became abnormal before packet delivery ratio declined, suggesting that queue occupancy or waiting time may provide earlier congestion information than packet loss alone.

This should be treated as motivation for later protocol design and testing, not yet as proof of protocol superiority.

---

# 15. Current Limitations

The current timed forwarding model is still deliberately controlled.

Current limitations include:

1. The simulated time unit is abstract and is not yet mapped to real milliseconds or seconds.
2. The source-to-relay stage is simplified.
3. Relay-to-destination forwarding uses a controlled fixed delay.
4. Link reliability and retry behavior are not yet fully integrated into the same discrete-event end-to-end pipeline.
5. Mobility and dynamic link availability are not yet part of the Day 3 experiment.
6. The shared-relay experiment is a controlled topology rather than a full random or mobile network.
7. Throughput support exists, but larger comparative throughput experiments still need to be run.

---

# 16. Next Steps

The next planned stage should extend B0 under dynamic network conditions.

Important next additions include:

```text
link failure during traffic
topology change during traffic
route invalidation during active forwarding
mobility-style link appearance/disappearance
temporary partition
reconnection
```

After the B0 dynamic-condition baseline is characterized, the project can move into dynamic multi-metric routing.

That phase can test whether routing decisions that consider metrics such as:

```text
queue load
queue delay
link reliability
expected retransmission cost
freshness
stability
```

can reduce congestion and improve destination-level delivery compared with hop-count-only B0.
